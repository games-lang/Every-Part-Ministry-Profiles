1. Personal Information
Keep this simple and ministry-relevant.
Include name, age range, phone, email, preferred contact method, family situation, transportation, occupation, languages spoken, and language proficiency.
I would avoid collecting more personal information than churches genuinely need.
2. About Me / Church Connection
This helps pastors understand the person’s relationship with the church.
Include how long they’ve attended, how connected they feel, how long they’ve followed Jesus, whether they are a member, which service or congregation they attend, previous church involvement, and prior serving experience.
For multicultural churches, I really like your idea of asking about connection across different services or communities when relevant.
3. Spiritual Gifts
This should identify gifts that may shape how someone naturally serves.
Examples could include teaching, mercy, helps, administration, leadership, hospitality, giving, encouragement, faith, evangelism, discernment, wisdom, knowledge, shepherding, service, and prayer/intercession.
I’d give people their top 3–5 gifts, not just one.
4. APEST
I think this is worth keeping because it answers a different question from spiritual gifts.
The five areas are Apostle, Prophet, Evangelist, Shepherd, and Teacher.
The result could show a primary and secondary APEST tendency rather than forcing someone into one category.
5. Personality / Working Style
You can keep a personality section, but for a commercial product I would be careful about directly using Myers-Briggs terminology unless you’re properly licensed to do so.
Long term, I’d probably create your own ministry-focused working-style assessment measuring things like structured vs. flexible, people-focused vs. task-focused, reflective vs. expressive, independent vs. collaborative, detail-oriented vs. big-picture, and steady vs. fast-paced.
That may actually be more useful for ministry placement than a generic personality type.
6. Passions
This is one of the most important sections.
Ask who or what they feel especially drawn toward, such as children, youth, young adults, families, new Christians, people who don’t know Jesus, immigrants/refugees, multicultural ministry, missions, people experiencing poverty, addiction recovery, grief, elderly adults, prayer, discipleship, worship, community outreach, justice/compassion, and second-generation ministry.
This helps answer: Who has God put on your heart?
7. Ministry Interests
Passions and interests should stay separate.
Someone may care deeply about youth but not want to teach a weekly youth class.
Ask what actual ministry areas they are interested in exploring, such as children, preschool, youth, young adults, worship, sound/tech, hospitality, greeting, prayer, small groups, discipleship, outreach, missions, communications, office/admin, finance, event planning, translation, transportation, maintenance, care ministry, or leadership.
8. Skills & Experience
This section can uncover abilities that spiritual-gifts tests miss.
Include profession, education, certifications, technical skills, creative skills, languages, music ability, teaching experience, leadership experience, financial skills, construction/trades, healthcare background, counseling experience, technology, cooking, driving, organization, and other unique abilities.
I would also ask:
What is something you're good at that your church may not know about?
That question could reveal a lot.
9. Life Experiences
I really like that your current profile includes this.
People often minister powerfully from experiences they’ve walked through.
Ask about experiences they are comfortable sharing that may help them minister to others, such as grief, parenting, foster/adoption, immigration, recovery, disability, caregiving, divorce, military service, cross-cultural experience, mission work, financial hardship, major career transitions, or other significant experiences.
Make this optional and clearly tell people not to share anything they don't want church leaders to know.
10. Availability & Serving Capacity
This is essential because a perfect ministry match is useless if the person cannot realistically serve there.
Include frequency, days/times available, seasonal availability, willingness to serve at special events, overnight retreats, mission trips, short-term projects, and whether they prefer ongoing or occasional serving.
I’d also ask:
How much serving responsibility feels realistic for you right now?
That could be extremely valuable.
11. Spiritual Health
I would keep this, but handle it carefully.
This should not feel like a pass/fail test or a tool for deciding who is “spiritual enough.”
Instead, assess areas such as prayer, Scripture, worship/church engagement, relationships, community/accountability, rest/Sabbath, serving motivation, emotional/spiritual well-being, and current sense of connection with God.
The goal should be to help pastors identify when someone may need care before responsibility, or when ministry participation could be part of healthy growth.
I like the concept you’ve already used of categories such as Needs Care, Growing, Healthy, Thriving, provided they’re framed pastorally rather than judgmentally.
12. Ministry Preferences & Environment
This is one I would add to your original profile.
Ask questions like:
Do you prefer working with people or behind the scenes?
Do you prefer leading or supporting?
Do you enjoy predictable routines or changing environments?
Do you prefer serving alone, with a small team, or with a large group?
Do you prefer relational ministry, practical service, teaching, administration, creative work, or outreach?
Would you rather serve weekly in one role or occasionally in several roles?
This could dramatically improve your ministry matching.
